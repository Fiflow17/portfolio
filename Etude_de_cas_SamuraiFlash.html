Case Study · Mobile Game · Data-Driven Product Design

SAMURAI FLASH

40M+ downloads · 36 prototypes killed · 1 scaled

In hypercasual, you do not get interviews, usability tests, or long feedback loops. You get a few days, paid acquisition data, and a kill-or-scale decision. This case study documents how I turned CPI, D1, D2/D7, and playtime into a decision system that helped scale Samurai Flash to 40M+ downloads.

Core Insight

A KPI is a symptom, not a cause. In a no-research environment, the designer's job is not to collect more data — it is to translate sparse performance signals into concrete hypotheses about player comprehension, friction, and motivation. That translation system is what made 36 kill decisions faster, cheaper, and defensible.

TL;DR — Operating model

36 prototypes killed.
1 scaled to 40M.

The shift was not about building better games in isolation. It was about building a decision system that treated KPIs as UX signals: CPI for concept legibility, D1 for onboarding clarity, D2/D7 for loop strength, and playtime for real engagement depth.

That system made design decisions faster, more defensible, and far more scalable under constant publisher pressure.

01 — Problem

Product design with
no qualitative feedback.

Each prototype was tested on thousands of real players over a few days. No one told us why something failed. No interviews. No usability sessions. No surveys. Only CTR, CPI, D1, D2/D7, and playtime.

My role was not to produce more data. It was to interpret incomplete signals fast enough to decide whether to simplify, iterate, or kill a concept before more time and acquisition budget were lost.

⚠ Constraint 01

The ad judges the game before the player does

CPI and CTR determine whether the concept is readable long before the product can reveal its deeper qualities. A concept that fails the ad never gets the chance to prove its loop.

⚠ Constraint 02

A good metric can still hide the wrong diagnosis

High CPI does not say whether the issue is creative, legibility, pacing, or mechanic framing. The same number can point to very different frictions, and misreading it wastes a full cycle.

⚠ Constraint 03

The system rewards speed, not certainty

Design choices had to be made before certainty was possible. Waiting for more evidence often meant killing momentum and burning acquisition spend with no actionable output.

⚠ Constraint 04

Acquisition and retention are tightly coupled

Misleading ads can improve clicks while destroying D1. Strong retention cannot compensate for a concept nobody understands in the ad. Both sides of the funnel must be aligned from the start.

02 — Diagnosis

Reading metrics
as player behavior.

Instead of reacting to numbers in isolation, I created a translation layer: each KPI mapped to a probable UX issue. That gave the team a faster, more structured way to decide what to test next, and stopped us from chasing the metric rather than the underlying friction.

Before — Intuition-led

  • Evaluate prototypes by what felt fun internally
  • React to bad numbers without a diagnosis layer
  • Rely on taste more than repeatable interpretation

✕ Fast, but too inconsistent to scale across 40+ prototypes.

After — Decision-driven

  • Map KPI signal → likely UX friction type
  • Generate a narrow, testable hypothesis set
  • Run targeted redesigns with a defined expected effect

✓ More rigorous, reusable, easier to defend under pressure.

03 — Ambiguity & Iteration

Good early signals
could still be wrong.

The hardest part was not reacting to obviously bad prototypes. It was dealing with the ones that looked promising at first, then broke later in the funnel. That is where judgment mattered most, and where the decision framework was tested for real.

What changed in practice

Strong CPI could no longer be treated as proof that a game deserved scale. I started evaluating every prototype against a stricter mass-market filter: snackable, readable in 2–5 seconds, satisfying, and forgiving. The system taught us to distrust early signals and delay conviction until D2/D7 held.

✕ Misleading signal

Ninja Slash — Good CPI, collapsed D7

CPI: strong · D1: acceptable · D7: failed

POV shooter with enemies directly in front of the player. Early signals were deceptively good. We tried unlockable weapons, rewards, and progression hooks.

▸ The issue was not progression. The core loop lacked physical impact: weapons felt weak, enemy feedback was poor, replay value never deepened. Good CPI was hiding a loop problem no surface feature could fix.

✖ Wrong fantasy at scale

Take a Selfie — Fun internally, wrong audience

Readability: strong · Internal reception: positive · Retention: failed

Ragdoll avoidance game about sneaking past bodyguards to reach a celebrity. Readable, funny, easy to grasp. Felt like a strong candidate internally.

▸ The game was built around avoidance and anxiety, not power or satisfaction. What scales in hypercasual is player fantasy: feeling capable, fast, or dominant. Avoidance is the wrong emotion.

Publisher pressure

Some publisher suggestions pushed toward deeper mechanic changes. My role was also to challenge that: weigh implementation time against the likely KPI upside, protect iteration speed, and kill concepts when the redesign cost exceeded the realistic gain. Speed was the asset. Protecting it was part of the design job.

04 — Key Decisions

Not features.
Operating decisions.

What mattered most was not adding content endlessly. It was identifying which design levers had the strongest, most direct relationship with each KPI signal, and executing on those with maximum speed.

My role in the loop

The publisher defined the performance thresholds. I turned CPI, D1, D7, and playtime into redesign hypotheses: what to simplify, what to test, and when to stop iterating. Every decision had to be justified against the signal, not against taste.

01Treat KPIs as UX signals, not final verdicts

Problem

Raw numbers alone could not explain why a prototype failed. Several very different UX issues could produce the same bad metric. Reaction without a translation layer meant wasted cycles.

Decision

Create a decision layer where CPI, D1, D2/D7, and playtime each pointed to a probable friction: hook legibility, onboarding, loop strength, or engagement depth.

Trade-off accepted

The interpretation remained indirect. The system improved reliability but did not eliminate misdiagnosis risk. Some decisions were still made under real ambiguity.

This is what made 36 kill decisions faster and cheaper: not more data, but a clearer reading framework that turned numbers into hypotheses.
02Prioritize visual legibility to reduce CPI

Problem

The gameplay worked, but CPI stayed high because the concept was too visually dense to read in under two seconds of a muted autoplay ad.

Decision

Simplify the environment, increase contrast, make the core action immediately legible at a glance — even without sound, even in the first second.

Trade-off accepted

We gave up visual richness and some premium feel in exchange for immediate comprehension. The game looked simpler. It converted better.

A/B testing confirmed the shift: CTR moved from 1.8% to 2.6%, CPI dropped from $0.48 to $0.29. Legibility is a business lever, not a compromise.
03Turn reward ads into progression tools

Problem

RV engagement was limited (~46% D0, ~1.7 impressions per user), indicating ads were seen as optional bonuses rather than meaningful accelerators.

Decision

Offer boosts, multipliers, and resources at moments of player desire: after success, near-failure, or just before a meaningful unlock.

Trade-off accepted

The game became more dependent on the ad model, which narrows monetization flexibility over time. The core loop now required the ad layer to feel complete.

This raised session depth and supported ~25 minutes average playtime, while improving ARPU/LTV potential and identifying a path toward the 60%+ RV target.
04Align acquisition creatives with real gameplay

Problem

Creative freedom on ads risked a promise mismatch: good click performance from staged footage, but low-quality cohorts and weak D1 from disappointed players.

Decision

Use only real gameplay moments in creatives: visible fails, tension, core loop satisfaction from the actual product — not a polished or staged version.

Trade-off accepted

This reduced marketing freedom significantly. The product had to carry the promise without staging or exaggeration.

Better D1/D2 stability and much safer scaling. Cohort quality improved alongside acquisition, making growth sustainable rather than fragile.
05 — Key Pivot

One A/B test changed
the design hierarchy.

The most important product shift was not a new mechanic. It was realizing that looking premium was actively hurting comprehension in acquisition. The data forced a choice between depth and readability — and readability won every time at scale.

▶ Design iteration — visual legibilityThe game that looked better performed worse.

V1 — Premium-first

Rich environments. High visual density.

We built early versions to look as strong as possible in isolation: richer environments, more visual detail, a more premium aesthetic that felt justified by the production effort.

Internal feedback was positive. The game looked good on its own terms.

✕ CPI: $0.48. CTR: 1.8%. The concept was not readable in the 2-second window of a muted autoplay ad. Visual complexity was hiding the core mechanic instead of communicating it.

V2 — Legibility-first

Stripped back. Core action immediately visible.

We simplified aggressively: cleaner environment, stronger contrast, fewer competing visual elements. The mechanic had to be readable in 2 seconds without sound and without context.

Internally, it felt like we were removing quality. The A/B test said otherwise.

✓ CPI: $0.29. CTR: 2.6%. The stripped version outperformed on every acquisition metric. Legibility was not a visual compromise — it was the product decision that unlocked scale.

This pivot restructured how we evaluated every subsequent prototype. Richness became a D7 problem, not an acquisition problem. Readability became non-negotiable from the first creative brief.
06 — Impact

Full-funnel alignment,
not one lucky metric.

Validation logic

The final proof was not just low CPI. Acquisition, activation, and engagement all moved in the same direction — which is what made Samurai Flash scalable rather than merely clickable. Single-metric wins are fragile. Funnel coherence is durable.

07 — Trade-offs

Every performance gain
had a cost.

Hypercasual success is built on simplifying aggressively and optimizing hard for the business model. Those decisions are effective — but they are not neutral. Each trade-off was made deliberately.

We traded visual richness for legibility.

A more stripped-back visual language drove CPI down to $0.29. It also reduced the feeling of depth compared to denser early versions. In ad-first environments, the hook is the product's first impression — and a confusing impression does not get a second chance.

We traded certainty for decision speed.

The KPI-to-friction model accelerated action significantly, but remained interpretive. Ninja Slash proved this: acceptable D1 was masking a loop problem the model did not detect until D7 collapsed. Some decisions were made under real ambiguity — and that was part of the system, not a failure of it.

We traded monetisation flexibility for ad-native engagement.

Rewarded ads worked because they were integrated into the progression loop rather than bolted on top. That also meant the core experience became dependent on the ad model to feel complete. More engaging with ads in — less coherent without them.

We traded depth for scalability.

Concepts were evaluated and killed within 48–72 hours. That speed protected budget and momentum, but meant some games with real potential were killed before a deeper iteration cycle could reveal their upside.

08 — Learnings

What this changed
in my design practice.

A KPI is a symptom. The job is to diagnose what caused it.

Ninja Slash was the clearest proof: good CPI and acceptable D1 still hid a loop that could not sustain D7. The number alone told us nothing. The reading framework told us where to look. Without the translation layer, we would have iterated on progression and missed the physical impact problem entirely.

Legibility is a business decision, not a visual compromise.

Stripping back V2 felt like reducing quality. The A/B test confirmed the opposite: CTR from 1.8% to 2.6%, CPI from $0.48 to $0.29. In ad-first environments, if the concept is not readable in 2 seconds without sound, the product does not get the chance to prove its depth. Readability is the prerequisite for everything else.

Acquisition and retention cannot be designed separately.

Take a Selfie proved this. The concept was readable, the ad acceptable — but the game was built around avoidance and anxiety rather than power and satisfaction. The cohort installed expecting one fantasy and got another. A strong ad that misrepresents the core loop creates expensive, low-quality cohorts that no retention fix can recover.

Designers in data-only environments are translators, not dashboards.

The value was not reading numbers. It was turning CPI into a hypothesis about hook legibility, D7 into a hypothesis about loop satisfaction, and running the right redesign in response. Speed of translation — from signal to hypothesis to test — was the core design skill. That is transferable, not hypercasual-specific.

Killing fast is a product skill.

36 prototypes were killed before Samurai Flash scaled. Each kill was a decision, not a failure. The system's value was not in finding the right game faster — it was in eliminating the wrong ones cheaply enough that the right one could receive full attention when it appeared.
